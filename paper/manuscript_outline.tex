\section*{Significance statememt}
Novel influenza diversity causes seasonal outbreaks, occasional pandemics, and hundreds of thousands of deaths annually, yet it remains difficult to predict the conditions where novel diversity will most likely emerge. This study reveals that colder local environmental temperatures are linked with faster evolution of influenza A surface proteins. However, the link between evolution and climate is not deterministic, such that there are no consistent molecular changes associated with colder climates. Therefore, the link is driven by stochastic factors such as virion transmission viability. By linking climate to evolutionary tempo across global data sets, we propose an intuitive explanation for seasonal and climatic viral epidemiological dynamics.

\section*{Abstract}
Influenza A displays marked seasonal patterns of infection in temperate regions, yet the extent to which local climates influence the emergence of novel viral diversity remains unknown. Cold and dry conditions promote viral transmission in laboratory settings, leading to an expected link between local climate and influenza A evolution. Here, we analysed a global sample of H1N1 and H3N2 influenza A genetic sequences isolated between 2009–2019, and test whether surface protein evolutionary rates are associated with local temperature, relative humidity, and population density. Our analyses show evidence for a negative association between the hemagglutinin surface protein evolutionary rate and environmental temperature across both influenza A subtypes. This link was link was observable in data for the epitope region of hemagglutinin, but also across the whole gene and across both synonymous and non-synonymous evolutionary change. Similarly, no mutations of a given kind or genetic location occur preferentially under particular conditions of environmental temperature or humidity. This likely reflects the effect of cold air on virion survival in droplets, and impaired host airway conditions, leading to looser transmission bottlenecks. The global nature of the data set establishes local temperature as a modulator of influenza A hemagglutinin evolutionary tempo, highlighting the importance of climate context when anticipating novel strains.


\section*{Introduction}

Influenza A is one of the most significant infectious diseases affecting humans, causing recurrent seasonal epidemics and sporadic pandemics (\cite{hsieh_influenza_2006, trovao_when_2020}). Infections display marked seasonal patterns, peaking in winter months in temperate regions and in rainy seasons in the tropics (\cite{petrova_evolution_2018}). Local climates may be involved in variant emergence through influencing modes of transmission, host immunity and respiratory tract conditions (\cite{kudo_low_2019}). One mechanism by which climate could influence viral evolution is through transmission bottlenecks. Specifically, climatic conditions that allow higher droplet stability can relax the population bottleneck at transmission (\cite{frise_contact_2016}), reducing the loss of novel variants during host-to-host spread. Experimental work has shown that colder and dry conditions increase transmission efficiency due to enhanced virus survival in airborne droplets (\cite{lowen_influenza_2007, shaman_absolute_2009}). Nonetheless, climatic drivers of influenza evolution are likely complex and context-dependent (\cite{deyle_global_2016}): while cold and dry conditions might enhance transmission, our ability to detect the effect on viral evolution will depend on how these factors interact with host immunity, population density, and sampling effort. The expansion of global influenza surveillance has led to extensive collections of viral genome sequences accompanied by sampling dates and locations. In particular, sequences of the antigenically important hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA) surface glycoproteins are routinely collected worldwide, providing an unprecedented opportunity to investigate environmental correlates of influenza evolutionary dynamics (\cite{volz_viral_2013}). 

The influenza surface proteins HA and NA are fast-evolving and central to infection and immune escape (\cite{nelson_evolution_2007}). HA mediates host cell entry by binding sialic acid receptors, while NA facilitates virion release by cleaving sialic acids from host glycoproteins (\cite{matsuoka_comprehensive_2013}). Both proteins mediate key stages of the viral life cycle that occur outside host cells and are sensitive to the physicochemical conditions that vary with local climate (\cite{klenow_influenza_2023, tosheva_hemagglutinin_2023}), raising the possibility that that climatic conditions influence the relative fitness of circulating HA and NA variants. Environmental temperature and humidity may further influence evolutionary rates indirectly, through altered transmission dynamics, infection duration, and viral loads. Alternatively, if evolution is dominated by the intracellular biochemical environment (\cite{partlow_influenza_2025}) or by non-climatic demographic patterns of contact and mobility (\cite{cauchemez_role_2011, endo_within_2021}), outdoor climate may be a weak or negligible predictor of surface protein evolutionary rates.

In this study, we test the expectation that climate impacts the evolutionary rate at immune-targeted sites of HA and NA in Influenza A. Specifically, climate is expected to be linked with antigenic drift, which is the continuous emergence of mutations from error-prone replication, and a potent driver of novel strain emergence and immune escape (\cite{bedford_integrating_2014}). Molecular genetic sequences of influenza A provide a framework for examining this process using phylogenetic methods that can reveal the selection pressure imposed by immune systems, mediated by the impact of population bottlenecks (\cite{nelson_evolution_2007, wille_ecology_2020}). Any such link between antigenic drift and environmental conditions should be observed in genetic surveillance data if some climates increase virion survival or host susceptibility. An additional process leading to substantial novelty in RNA viruses is antigenic shift, but its link with climate is poorly defined since this process depends on co-infection by multiple circulating viral lineages that undergo genetic reassortment inside a host (\cite{nelson_evolution_2007}).

Using a global dataset of influenza A sequences with information on location and timing of collection across the two dominant seasonal subtypes, H1N1 and H3N2, we test for an association between rates of molecular evolution of surface antigens and local climatic temperature and humidity, as well as with local population density. Our tests of the impact of climate on pathogen emergence add renewed value to global genomic surveillance efforts, and refine our prospects of pandemic preparedness under climate change, where seasonal epidemics might transform into more persistent or geographically diffuse outbreaks (\cite{baker_implications_2021}).

\section*{Results}

To assess whether local climate conditions shape influenza A molecular evolution, we applied phylogenetic multiple regression to data of substitution rates estimated from sequences of H1N1 and H3N2, collected between 2009 and 2019 across 168 countries (Figure \ref{fig:main}A). The surface proteins HA and NA are crucial targets for this analysis for being fast-evolving and playing a central role in immune escape, while the internal polymerase acidic protein (PA) served as a negative control. Using between 8,000 and 15,000 sequences per gene segment (totalling over $\sim$60,000 across six segments), we estimated for each sample overall gene molecular rates as well as synonymous and non-synonymous rates in a phylogenetic analysis framework. We then linked each sample location to ERA5-Land climate data on monthly temperature and humidity and demographic data of population density (see Methods). Phylogenetic multiple regression was then used to test whether HA, NA and PA evolutionary rates are explained by local temperature, humidity, and additional covariates. Estimates of synonymous and non-synonymous molecular change were used to further test whether any link with climate is restricted to overall gene evolutionary rates or with changes associated with signatures of selection. To account for the uneven seasonal distribution of influenza surveillance, we also included sampling density as a covariate, defined as the number of samples per location per month and year.

Molecular dating analyses of our curated global data sets of the influenza A strains H1N1 and H3N2 revealed a strong measurably evolving signal, also known as temporal signal (Figure \ref{fig:main}B), and rate estimates consistent with previous work (\cite{phyu_evolutionary_2022, al_khatib_evolution_2018}). Specifically, data of H1 showed a signal of slightly higher evolutionary rates than H3 (0.00375 [95\% CI: 0.0004–0.0052] vs. 0.00326 [0.0004–0.0044] substitutions/site/year), and slightly lower variation in rates across lineages ($R^2$ H1N1 = 0.93 versus $R^2$ H3N2 = 0.84). Regression modelling to test for predictors of surface protein evolutionary rates showed substantial statistical support for including climatic variables in the model (Figure \ref{fig:main}C; $\Delta AIC \gg 2$). Additionally, we found evidence from these models that temperature is negatively associated with HA substitution rate, and this was observed in the separate analyses for the H1 and H3 subtypes (Figure \ref{fig:main}C). However, this effect was not observed in analyses of the NA or PA evolutionary rates.

To examine whether environmental temperature is linked with the selection regime of hemagglutinin rather than the overall mutation supply, we analysed synonymous ($d_{\text{S}}$) and non-synonymous ($d_{\text{N}}$) substitution rates separately across the whole gene, the stalk region (HA2), epitope regions, and the antigenic ridge of the gene (Figure \ref{fig:s1_map}). The regression coefficients for $d_{\text{N}}$, $d_{\text{S}}$, and overall substitution rate were consistent in direction and magnitude across the H1 and H3 subtypes, and indicate that temperature modulates the overall rate of molecular change without shifting the balance between positive and purifying selection (Figure \ref{fig:main}D; Figure \ref{fig:s2_epiforest}). The temperature signal persisted across all gene regions of H1 but was not present in the subregion analyses for H3 after correction for false discovery rates (Table \ref{tab:s1_epitope_p}).

Regression analyses of evolutionary rates can be notoriously noisy or impacted by subsets of the data, such that we performed subsampling stability analyses to test the robustness of the results. These analyses indicated that the temperature signal in hemagglutinin was not driven by small number of sequences or sequence clusters. Indeed, the proportion of significant subsamples increased with draw size (Figure \ref{fig:s3_jk_sizes}; Figure \ref{fig:s4_jk_sequence}), suggesting that a large dataset may be important for reliably detecting this signal. When drawing subsamples of 60\% of the dataset, temperature retained evidence of a negative effect on substitution rate in 99.7\% of subsamples for H1 rates and 96.5\% for H3 rates. (Figure \ref{fig:main}E).

The 2009 H1N1 pandemic has been considered a major source of novel diversity in influenza, such that we explored its possible impact in the observed stronger signal of the association between H1 rates and environmental temperature. Specifically, we performed analyses excluding the small portion of sequences sampled during the pandemic (2009-2010; 3\% of the data; Figure \ref{fig:s5_pandemicforest}). We found that the signal was present and of comparable magnitude in the post-pandemic dataset, confirming that sequences from the pandemic do not drive the result. The small portion of data from the pandemic do not show a signature of a link between H1 evolutionary rates and environmental temperature.

Analysis of non-synonymous mutations in the antigenic ridge and epitope regions of H1 and H3 revealed that several amino acid substitutions have arisen independently multiple times across the phylogeny (Figure \ref{fig:mutations}, top row). However, we did not find evidence of any association between an individual substitution type and any climate variable after correction for false discovery rates (Figure \ref{fig:mutations}, bottom row). This suggests that cold or dry conditions are not associated with selection for specific amino acid changes at the antigenic surface.

\section*{Discussion}

In a large global sample of influenza A H1N1 and H3N2 sequences, we reveal a strong negative association between local environmental temperature and hemagglutinin evolutionary rate, a finding that is robust to subsampling and consistent across both subtypes. Prior work has established that cold, dry conditions enhance influenza transmission by prolonging infectious droplet survival in the air and increasing contact rates indoors (\cite{lowen_influenza_2007, frise_contact_2016}), but a direct link between climate and the rate of molecular evolution of circulating strains has not been demonstrated at a global scale. Our evidence that cold local climates are linked with accelerated HA evolution is stronger with larger sample sizes, highlighting the value of global scale genomics surveillance efforts for understanding the nuanced causes of evolutionary novelty in pathogens with pandemic potential.

The result of increasing HA evolutionary rates in colder climates is consistent with several biological mechanisms, which might also converge as the root causes. Cold and dry conditions also allow virions to persist in aerosol for longer, and impair innate antiviral defences (\cite{lowen_influenza_2007, kudo_low_2019}). These factors allow for a wider effective transmission bottleneck (\cite{sobel_leonard_transmission_2017}), such that a larger proportion of the viral diversity in a host can be transmitted and retained across successive infections. This can lead to an elevated substitution rate at the population level in cold climates as observed in our data, without altering the per-replication error rate, or the balance between positive and purifying selection. This is consistent with the absence of evidence of a temperature effect indicators of selection such as on $\omega$ in our data, nor in over-represented amino acid changes (\ref{fig:mutations}). Antigenic selection is therefore likely dominated by immune escape (\cite{smith_mapping_2004}) in which competing antigenic lineages are resolved by host population immunity rather than environmental conditions (\cite{strelkowa_clonal_2012}).

Cold and dry conditions make mucociliary transport slower (\cite{kelly_rapid_2021, kilgour_mucociliary_2004}) and reduce the pH in airway surfaces (\cite{kudo_low_2019}). This means that virions are exposed to the extracellular environment for longer, also having to resist inactivation before reaching the respiratory epithelium. This is highly relevant to novel HA variants because the activation of the protein is is strongly pH-dependent. Variants that activate at lower pH thresholds are more stable in mildly acidic extracellular environments, conferring a selective transmission advantage (\cite{russier_molecular_2016, singanayagam_influenza_2019}). However, hemagglutinin stability across pH is complex and factors other than the gene variants play a role (\cite{yang_relationship_2021}). Excessive acidification can also prematurely trigger HA and render virions non-infectious (\cite{tosheva_hemagglutinin_2023}). Therefore, while our results do not point to strong selection constraints in hemagglutinin, they could still be explained by filtering of the viral population in airways for pH-stable variants.

The impact of climate on influenza evolution is present in HA data but absent from our data of the neuraminidase and PA proteins. This is likely explained by HA being the most rapidly evolving influenza gene and the primary target of neutralising antibody responses (\cite{bush_positive_1999, nelson_evolution_2007}). This means that HA has the greatest capacity to accumulate variation that is not immediately purged by purifying selection. Additionally, HA evolution is affected by extracellular conditions such as pH as explained above, while NA mediates virion release from already-infected cells (\cite{guo_kinetic_2018}), with negligible involvement in extracellular environments. The null result in PA rates confirms that the pipeline is not generating spurious climate associations in slowly evolving genes.

The association between temperature and evolutionary rate was observed in both subtypes, but strong evidence is more consistent in H1N1 than H3N2 across analyses of gene subregions and subsampling stability. The differences between the subtypes that might underlie this result include the lower overall evolutionary rate in H3N2, which can reduce our power to detect the impact of climate. The H3N2 subtype also has greater rate heterogeneity across lineages, suggesting it is more susceptible than H1N1 to changes in evolutionary rate that can dilute the signal of climate. A less ladder-like phylogenetic tree topology in H3N2 also suggests weaker selective constraints, suggesting that factors other than climate that weaken transmission bottlenecks play a greater role in H3N2 than in H1N1 (Figure \ref{fig:s7_tree}). Nonetheless, the presence of the result across both subtypes suggests that the underlying mechanisms described by climate are strong enough to consistently appear as dominant predictors of evolutionary rates.

An inherent trade-off of working with global surveillance data is that large scale data across broad geographic and temporal scales are important for detecting the signal of climate, but precludes the fine-grained resolution needed to verify the underlying mechanism. In particular, transmission and evolutionary dynamics are shaped by infectious periods and social contact networks (\cite{cauchemez_role_2011, khurana_high-resolution_2024}), and these aggregate out entirely at the resolution of our data. Global genomic surveillance datasets such as GISAID are also geographically uneven, with submissions heavily concentrated in North America, Europe, and East Asia. This concentration means that our results might not hold for tropical settings (\cite{kimura_decoding_2026}), could inflate the apparent climate signal or introduce geographic confounders that the sampling density covariate only partially addresses. Collection year is also a proxy for a period of replacement of lineages and was used as a fixed effect, but this decision represents the trade-off with the likely far greater complexities of within-subtype trends. Similarly, fine resolution mobility data would be a meaningful improvement to population density data, which was derived from the GHSL at 5-year epochs.

Using a global sample of influenza A H1N1 and H3N2 sequences spanning a decade, we demonstrate a robust negative association between local environmental temperature and hemagglutinin evolutionary rate. This effect is consistent in direction across subtypes, gene regions, and subsamples, and absent in neuraminidase and the internal control PA. Our data suggests that cold climates accelerate HA evolutionary rates without shifting the balance of selection, consistent with a mechanism of wider transmission bottlenecks rather than altered polymerase fidelity or directional immune pressure. This establishes cold local climate as a condition that raises the probability of novel variant emergence by increasing the pace of molecular change. Examining the mechanism at a finer molecular and geographic scale will be a valuable future direction of research, and might benefit from the use of data from large-scale population registries (\cite{khurana_high-resolution_2024}). As climate changes seasonally and with global change, so too may the geographic distribution of evolutionary hotspots, and the integration of genomic surveillance with environmental data offers a powerful framework for understanding and anticipating pathogen emergence.


\section*{Methods}

Molecular sequences of the HA, NA, and PA genes from the H1N1 and H3N2 influenza subtypes ranging collection dates between 2009 and 2019 were obtained from GISAID EpiFlu (\cite{shu_gisaid_2017}). The geographic coordinates of collection were assigned by geocoding the location strings in the GISAID metadata via Nominatim (OpenStreetMap) (\cite{noauthor_architecture_nodate, noauthor_openstreetmap_nodate}), using coarser geographic and political levels as a fallback when precise geocoding failed. For each sample, monthly mean air temperature and dewpoint temperature at 2-metres above the ground were extracted with a resolution from the ERA5-Land reanalysis at ${\sim}0.1^\circ$ spatial resolution via the Copernicus Climate Data Store (\cite{noauthor_era5_nodate}). Relative humidity was then derived from these using the Magnus formula (\cite{alduchov_improved_1996}. These two variables of temperature and humidity had limited correlation between them, such that they reflect distinct climatic signals ($R^2 < 0.01$). Differences in temperature ($\Delta$T) and relative humidity ($\Delta$RH) were also computed, and are calculated as the difference between the collection month and the preceding month at the same location coordinates of collection. Population density was extracted from the Global Human Settlement Layer (GHSL R2023A, 100 m resolution, Mollweide projection) at the geocoded coordinates for each sample, with the GHSL epoch (2010/2015/2020) (\cite{pesaresi_advances_2024}) matched to the collection year. Sampling density, defined as the count of sequences in the dataset sharing the same location and collection date (year, month), was included as a covariate to control for uneven global surveillance effort.

Sequences for each gene (HA, NA, and PA) and subtype (H1N1 and H3N2) were aligned with MAFFT v7.526 (\cite{katoh_mafft_2013}), leading to six sequence alignments that were the focus of subsequent analyses.  Alignment columns with $>$50\% gaps and duplicate sequences were removed prior to phylogenetic inference (Table \ref{tab:sequences}). The final alignments of H1, H3, N1, N2, PA, and PA contained 1,737, 1,728, 1,419, 1,410, 2,175, 2,184 nucleotide sites and 10,918, 15,002, 9,575, 13,054, 8,821, 12,892 sequences, respectively. For each of the six alignments, an initial phylogeny and branch lengths in expected substitutions per site was inferred using the approximate maximum likelihood method implemented in MAPLE v0.6.8 (\cite{de_maio_maximum_2022}). We then inferred divergence times for each of the six alignments, using collection dates as tip calibrations under a relaxed clock and approximate maximum likelihood molecular dating as implemented in TreeTime (\cite{sagulenko_treetime_2018}). Sequences whose root-to-tip divergence deviated from the molecular clock regression by more than 4 interquartile ranges were identified as unrealistic rates that are difficult to estimate and excluded from the alignment using the ape R package (\cite{ape_2019}). A second MAPLE tree was inferred from the clock-filtered dataset and then time-calibrated with TreeTime under the same settings. Evolutionary rates per alignment were then extracted for each phylogenetic tree branch as the ratio of branch length in expected substitutions per site (inferred in MAPLE) to time duration (inferred in TreeTime).

The large number of highly similar short sequences makes maximum likelihood inference of synonymous ($d_\text{S}$) and non-synonymous ($d_\text{N}$) branch lengths numerically unstable, such that we used a surrogate method based parsimony-based ancestral sequence reconstruction. First, codon columns containing stop codons or gaps were removed prior to $d_\text{N}$ and $d_\text{S}$ branch length inference. Maximum parsimony ancestral sequences were inferred using the phangorn R package (\cite{schliep_phangorn_2011}), followed by counting of the synonymous and non-synonymous changes across the sequence observed from phylogenetic tips to their ancestral terminal node. Terminal branch lengths were then taken to be the total number of synonymous or non-synonymous changes inferred in this framework, and divided by their corresponding time duration in the chronogram to infer $d_\text{N}$ and $d_\text{S}$ rates, along with $\omega$. Samples with remaining numerically unstable or extremely small rates (log rate $< -$8) were taken to be difficult to estimate and excluded from subsequent analyses (Table \ref{tab:sequences}, column `Final').

Since adaptive evolution in hemagglutinin  occurs primarily in antigenic sites of gene sub-regions (\cite{bush_positive_1999}), non-synonymous substitution rates were also estimated separately for the antigenic ridge, the broader epitope regions, and the HA2 stalk region. These subregions were identified and extracted by mapping the annotations from the H1 reference sequence A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 (CY009444) and H3 reference sequence A/Aichi/2/1968 (CY12168) (\cite{bao_influenza_2008}) from the complete MAFFT alignment (Figure \ref{fig:s1_map}). Given that gene subregions are too short for reliable maximum likelihood inference of pairwise distances, inference of synonymous and non-synonymous pairwise distances was performed using maximum parsimony using phangorn (\cite{schliep_phangorn_2011}). Stop codons, gaps, and ambiguous bases were excluded. The per-tip non-synonymous rate was once again calculated as the mapped pairwise distances to be non-synonymous tip lengths in the sub-region, divided by the corresponding chronogram branch length. Sequences lacking any non-synonymous change in the analysed region lead to rate estimates of zero, which have infinite variance in standard frameworks of rate inference (\cite{sanderson_estimating_2002, angelis_bayesian_2014}) and were excluded from subregion analyses (Table \ref{tab:s2_epitope}).

Phylogenetic multiple linear regression models were fitted while estimating Pagel's $\lambda$ to account for phylogenetic signal in the residuals, as implemented in the phylolm R package (\cite{ho_phylolm_2020}),. Whole-gene analyses were performed with the response variable being log overall evolutionary rate or log $\omega$, where $\omega = d_\text{N} / d_\text{S}$. Subregion analyses used log non-synonymous substitution rate for each region (antigenic ridge, epitope regions, HA2) as the response. All models included the covariates of temperature ($^\circ$C), relative humidity (\%), $\Delta$-temperature, $\Delta$-humidity, log sampling density, log population density, and collection year as a factor to control for any striking trends across years. Benjamini--Hochberg FDR correction was applied to all phylolm results, and associations are reported both using their statistical significance ($p_\text{FDR} < 0.05$) and regression coefficient and its uncertainty as effect size.

To assess the robustness of the temperature association to subsampling, a subsample stability analysis was performed. Year-stratified random subsamples of either 1000 sequences, 15\%, 30\% or 60\% of the samples were taken 1000 times each. The phylogeny was pruned to each subsample and regression models were refitted with log substitution rate (or log non-synonymous rate for subregion analyses) as the response and scaled temperature and collection year as predictors. The distribution of temperature coefficients and confidence intergals across subsamples, and the proportion reaching $p < 0.05$, are reported as a test for the full-model signal being attributable to any subset of sequences or temporal cluster.

Recurrent non-synonymous substitutions were taken as those between the same two amino acids at a given genetic location. Those occurring at least five times independently across tips of the phylogeny were tested for association between event counts and the climate variable at the corresponding sample location using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with effect size quantified as the rank-biserial correlation. Benjamini--Hochberg FDR correction was applied within each gene and region. All data handling, statistical analyses and visualisations were performed in R v4.5.1 (\cite{r_2025}) in the tidyverse (\cite{tidyverse_2019}) ecosystem. Climate and population density covariates were appended to sequence metadata using Python v3.12.9 (\cite{vanrossum_python_2009}) with the xarray (\cite{hoyer2017xarray}) and pandas (\cite{reback2020pandas}) libraries.
