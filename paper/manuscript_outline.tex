\section*{Introduction}

\subsection*{Influenza A epidemiology and climate}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Influenza A shows marked seasonal patterns: winter peaks in temperate
        regions, rainy-season peaks in the tropics; occasional pandemics from
        major genomic change. [Petrova \& Russell 2018; Trovão \& Nelson 2020;
        Hsieh et al.\ 2006]
  \item Local climates influence variant emergence via transmission mode, host
        immunity, and respiratory tract conditions. [Kudo et al.\ 2019]
  \item Colder, drier conditions increase droplet stability, relaxing the
        population bottleneck at transmission [Frise et al.\ 2016; Lowen et al.\
        2007]; hot/humid conditions reduce desiccation [Minhaz Ud-Dean 2010]
        --- the overall climate effect on evolution is complex and poorly
        understood. [Deyle et al.\ 2016]
  \item Surveillance data (HA + NA sequences with date and location) provide a
        window into strain emergence even without patient-level metadata.
        [Volz et al.\ 2013]
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Surface proteins HA and NA as evolutionary targets}
\begin{itemize}
  \item HA binds sialic acids (viral entry); NA cleaves sialic acids (virion
        release) --- both are fast-evolving and central to transmission
        efficiency. [Nelson \& Holmes 2007; Matsuoka et al.\ 2013]
  \item Function is acutely sensitive to extracellular conditions (pH, mucus
        composition, temperature). [Tosheva et al.\ 2023; Klenow et al.\ 2023]
  \item Variants with lower HA activation pH have a selective advantage in
        mildly acidic extracellular environments. [Russier et al.\ 2016;
        Singanayagam et al.\ 2019]
  \item Cold/dry conditions reduce mucociliary transport velocity
        [Kilgour et al.\ 2004; Kelly et al.\ 2021] and lower airway pH
        [Kullmann et al.\ 2008] --- the airway is therefore a climate-sensitive
        site of selection on surface proteins.
  \item Dry air also impairs innate antiviral defence and tissue repair,
        potentially increasing susceptibility to infection. [Kudo et al.\ 2019]
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Antigenic drift and climate}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Antigenic drift (mutation accumulation under immune pressure) is a
        primary mechanism of novel strain emergence [Nelson \& Holmes 2007;
        Wille \& Holmes 2020]; antigenic shift (reassortment) is a separate,
        episodic process not the focus here.
  \item Genetic draft: climate-driven selection could carry neutral synonymous
        mutations, explaining any signal in overall rate without a corresponding
        $\omega$ signal. [Gillespie 2000]
  \item Warm tropical/subtropical regions contribute disproportionately to
        global influenza A diversity; H3N2 shows particularly fast antigenic
        drift and geographic diffusion despite lower seasonal amplitude.
        [Baker et al.\ 2021; Rambaut et al.\ 2008]
  \item Global climate change may expand evolutionary hotspots and shift
        seasonal dynamics toward year-round transmission --- motivating a
        quantitative test of the climate--evolution link. [Baker et al.\ 2021]
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Gaps and study rationale}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Previous work (including our own earlier analysis) was limited by:
        use of static climatology (WorldClim) rather than time-matched climate
        data; NCBI sequences without GISAID-level quality control; no internal
        protein negative control to distinguish true signal from noise.
  \item This study uses a GISAID dataset with time-matched ERA5-Land climate
        data (monthly $\times$ $\sim$0.1\textdegree{} resolution) and includes
        PA (polymerase acidic protein, internal) as a negative control.
  \item \textbf{Study aim:} Test whether local temperature, relative humidity,
        and population density predict molecular evolutionary rates (overall
        rate, dN/dS) in HA and NA of H1N1 and H3N2 using phylogenetic
        comparative methods, and compare results against an internal-protein
        negative control.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Sequence data}
\begin{itemize}
  \item GISAID EpiFlu: HA (H1, H3) and NA (N1, N2) genes for H1N1 and H3N2;
        PA for both subtypes as negative control. \textcolor{noteblue}{[REF: GISAID]}
  \item \textit{[Add time range and approximate N per gene once finalised.]}
  \item Metadata: collection date (month resolution), location
        (country/region string).
  \item Split FASTAs merged and metadata parsed with
        \texttt{scripts/00\_metadata/00\_parse\_gisaid.R}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Climate data}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Location strings geocoded to lat/lon via Nominatim (OpenStreetMap)
        with hierarchical fallback to coarser geographic levels; results cached
        in \texttt{data/raw/location\_coords\_cache.csv}.
        \textcolor{noteblue}{[REF: Nominatim / OpenStreetMap]}
  \item ERA5-Land monthly means fetched from the Copernicus CDS
        (\texttt{scripts/00\_metadata/02\_attach\_climate.py}): 2-m temperature
        and 2-m dewpoint at sample location and collection month.
        \textcolor{noteblue}{[REF: ERA5-Land / Copernicus CDS --- old paper ref 41]}
  \item Relative humidity derived from temperature and dewpoint using the
        Magnus formula; temperature converted from Kelvin to Celsius.
        \textcolor{noteblue}{[REF: Magnus formula]}
  \item Population density extracted from the Global Human Settlement Layer
        (GHSL R2023A, 100\,m resolution, Mollweide projection); epoch matched
        to sample collection year (2010/2015/2020 layers available).
        \textcolor{noteblue}{[REF: GHSL --- Schiavina et al.\ or JRC technical report; check GHSL\_Data\_Package\_2023.pdf]}
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Sequence alignment and phylogenetics}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Codon-aware pairwise alignment using MACSE v2.07 (batch $\times$ batch
        to manage runtime); columns with $>$50\% missing data excluded.
        \textcolor{noteblue}{[REF: MACSE --- old paper ref 47]}
  \item Initial phylogeny inferred with MAPLE (fast ML, pandemic-scale).
        \textcolor{noteblue}{[REF: MAPLE --- old paper ref 45]}
  \item Two-round pruning: round 1 removes near-zero-length branches (likely
        identical/duplicate sequences); round 2 removes outlier-long branches
        (likely sequencing errors or mis-assignments). Thresholds set in
        \texttt{config.R}.
  \item Molecular dating with TreeTime using sampling times as calibrations to
        obtain a timetree with branch lengths in years.
        \textcolor{noteblue}{[REF: TreeTime --- old paper ref 46]}
  \item Overall per-sample evolutionary rate $=$ terminal branch length
        (substitutions) $\div$ branch duration (years).
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{dN/dS estimation}
\begin{itemize}
  \item MACSE codon alignments used for pairwise dN and dS estimation via
        codeml (PAML), HKY$+\Gamma$ and F3$\times$4 models.
        \textcolor{noteblue}{[REF: PAML/codeml --- old paper refs 49, 50; HKY+$\Gamma$ model; F3$\times$4 codon model]}
  \item Branch lengths in dN and dS fitted from pairwise distances onto the
        MAPLE tree topology via NNLS (\texttt{phangorn::nnls.tree}).
        \textcolor{noteblue}{[REF: phangorn --- old paper ref 51]}
  \item $\omega$ (dN/dS) rate per sample $=$ (dN/dS) $\div$ branch duration.
  \item Samples with near-zero or numerically unstable rates excluded
        (log rate $< -8$).
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Phylogenetic comparative regression}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Phylogenetic generalised linear model via \texttt{phylolm} (Brownian
        motion covariance); Pagel's $\lambda$ estimated during regression.
        \textcolor{noteblue}{[REF: phylolm --- old paper ref 52]}
  \item Response variables: (1) log overall evolutionary rate,
        (2) log $\omega$ rate.
  \item Predictors: temperature, relative humidity, population density, and
        all two-way interactions; interactions dropped if none significant.
  \item Analyses run independently per gene
        (H1, H3, N1, N2, PA\_H1N1, PA\_H3N2).
  \item Population density subsets (urban vs.\ rural, threshold TBD) analysed
        in addition to the full dataset.
  \item PA genes serve as negative control: no climate signal in PA
        strengthens inference of genuine surface-protein-specific effects.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Results --- placeholder structure}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Sample sizes and geographic/temporal coverage per gene
        (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}).
  \item Phylogenetic regression coefficients: temperature, humidity, and
        sampling density per gene for log terminal branch rate, with PA as
        negative control (Fig.~\ref{fig:forest}).
  \item Consistency of the temperature effect across genomic regions: terminal
        branch rate, full-gene NS and S rates, epitope NS rate, and ridge NS
        rate (Fig.~\ref{fig:multilevel}).
  \item Non-synonymous and synonymous rates are both negatively associated with
        temperature, supporting a mutational rather than purely selective
        mechanism (Fig.~\ref{fig:nsvss}).
  \item Urban/rural subset results.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Discussion --- placeholder points}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Interpret direction and magnitude of climate associations (and how they
        differ from the previous analysis).
  \item Mechanistic hypothesis: climate effects on mucus viscosity, airway pH,
        or transmission bottleneck as mediators.
  \item Genetic draft vs.\ direct selection as explanation for $\omega$ vs.\
        overall rate patterns.
  \item Limitations: uneven global surveillance, missing neuraminidase
        inhibitor data, people-movement noise, population density not yet
        integrated.
  \item Climate change implications for influenza A evolution and surveillance.
\end{itemize}
