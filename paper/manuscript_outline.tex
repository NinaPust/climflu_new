\section*{Introduction}

%\subsection*{Influenza A epidemiology and climate}
Influenza A infections display marked seasonal patterns, peaking in winter months in temperate regions and in rainy seasons in the tropics (\cite{petrova_evolution_2018}), also causing devastating epidemics and most of the major pandemics in the last century (\cite{hsieh_influenza_2006, trovao_when_2020}). Local climates are likely to be involved in variant emergence by influencing mode of transmission, host immunity and respiratory tract conditions (\cite{kudo_low_2019}). Climatic conditions that allow greater droplet stability can relac the population bottleneck at transmission (\cite{frise_contact_2016}), reducing the loss of novel variants during host-to-host spread. Experimental work has shown that colder and dried conditions increase transmission efficiency due to enhanced virus survival in airborne droplets (\cite{lowen_influenza_2007, shaman_absolute_2009}). Meanwhile, hot and humid conditions might also promote viral survival by reducing dessication (\cite{minhaz_ud-dean_structural_2010}), such that the role of climate on variant emergence is likely complex (\cite{deyle_global_2016}) and remains poorly understood. Modern global-scale surveillance data frequently includes genetic sequences for the core hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA) viral surface protein genes, which provide fundamental information about strain emergence in addition to date and location of collection, even in the abselnce of additional patient information (\cite{volz_viral_2013}).  

The influenza viral surface proteins HA and NA are fast-evolving and directly involved in transmission and infection efficiency (\cite{nelson_evolution_2007}). HA binds to sialic acid on host glycoproteins or glycolipids, facilitationg viral entry into the host cell. Meanwhile, NA cleaves sialic acids on host and virion glycoproteins, enabling the release of virions from the host cells (\cite{matsuoka_comprehensive_2013}). The function od both od these surface proteins can be acutely affected by extracellular environmental conditions, in particular pH (\cite{klenow_influenza_2023, tosheva_hemagglutinin_2023}), pointing to strong selection pressures prior to cell access. Variants that require a lower pH for the activation of HA are thought to have a selective advantage in transmission, as they are less susceptible to inactivation in mildly acidic extracellular environments (\cite{russier_molecular_2016, singanayagam_influenza_2019, tosheva_hemagglutinin_2023}). Meanwhile, colder and drier conditions reduce mucus transport velocity (\cite{kelly_rapid_2021, kilgour_mucociliary_2004}) and likely pH in airways (\cite{kullmann_environmental_2008}). 

Dry air additionally impairs innate antiviral defence and tissue repair in mice, making regions with driest climates likely to worsen susceptibility to severe disease (\cite{kudo_low_2019}) . Environmental temperature and humidity might therefore impact viral evolution via either changed infection times, viral loads, and selection pressures mediated by factors like airway mucus composition. Alternatively, outdoor environmental climate might not be relecant predictor of viral novelty. This might occur if evolution is dominated by intracellular environment (\cite{partlow_influenza_2025}) or by demographic patterns of transmission driven by social habits and networks (\cite{cauchemez_role_2011, endo_within_2021}). 

In RNA viruses like influenza A, mutational genetic change, known as antigenic drift, is shaped by multiple factors including mutation rates, replication dynamics, and host immune pressures (\cite{nelson_evolution_2007, wille_ecology_2020}). Antigenic drift, influenced by host immunity and vaccination, is a potent driver of novel strain emergence in influenza (\cite{bedford_integrating_2014}), along with reassortment events between co-occurring lineages (i.e., antigenic shift) (\cite{nelson_evolution_2007}). Additionally, antigenic drift reduces vaccine eddicacy and maintains some regions as global virus reservoirs (\cite{naeem_antigenic_2020}). Environmental conditions can influenxe evolutionary rates either directly via physical conditions for virion survival, or indirectly by altering social dynamics and people movement. Global climate change might therefore alter the prevalebce and geographic distribution of evolutionary hotspots, likely causing more recurrent or ongoing outbreaks compared to the seasonal peaks currently observed in temperate regions (\cite{baker_implications_2021}). Such changes elevate the risk of novel strain emergence, underscoring the need to test the importance of climate for viral evolution. 

In this study, we use a global data set of influenza A to test for the links between local climate, population density, and rates of molecular evolution of surface antigens in two most common seasonal strains; H1N1 and H3N2. Additionally, we use the polymerase acidic protein gene (PA), an internal, slow-evolving gene, as a negative control. Using a dataset with over \textbf{[X]} surveillance samples of each of the genes, sampled globally across two strains, we inferred terminal branch rates and non-synonymous and synonymous substitution rates per sample. We then drew from global climatic and demographic databases to prepare a per-sample dataset of time-stamped temperature, humidity, and population density. Comparative analysis was then performed on these data to test for fundamental climatic or demographic predictors of evolutionary rates. By characterizing how climate influences viral evolutionary dynamics, we propose hypotheses for the formation of influenza A evolutionary hotspots and seasonal peaks under global climatic change.  

\section*{Results}

To asses whether local climate conditions shape influenza A evolution, we applied phylogenetic comparative regression to substitution rates estimated across H1N1 and H3N2 sequences spanning between 2009 and 2019 across 168 countries (Figure \ref{fig:main}A). Surface proteins HA and NA were tested due to their fast-evolving nature and key role in immune escape, while PA served as a negative control. 

To account for the confounding seasonal burden of influenza A infections in winter months, sampling density was added as a response. The results reveal that HA sequences collected at higher temperature show a decreased substitution rate (Figure \ref{fig:main}C). This effect was not observed in NA and PA.

To further look into the impact of temperature on HA substitution rate, the rate of synonymous ($d_{\text{S}}$) and non-synonymous ($d_{\text{N}}$) change was analysed on the whole gene, the stalk region (HA2), the epitope regions and the antigenic ridge (\textcolor{red}{Supp figure gene map}). No notable difference was observed between the $d_{\text{N}}$, $d_{\text{S}}$ and overall substitution rates across both analysed subtypes (Figure \ref{fig:main}D), implying that temperature plays a role in mutational rate, but doesn't influence selection. Analyses on the stalk region (HA2), epitope regions and the antigenic ridge showed that the temperature signal persists across gene regions of H1, while H3 does not reach significance on these analyses. This could be due to the reduced statistical power of only taking sequences that have undergone a non-synonymous change in each respective gene region, along with the fact that the overall regression analysis was less significant in H3N2 than in H1N1 ($p_{\text{FDR(H1N1)}} = value$, $p_{\text{FDR(H3N2)}} = value$).

\section*{Discussion}

In a large global sample of influenza A H1N1 and H3N2 sequences, we reveal a robust negative association between local environmental temperature and haemagglutinin evolutionary rate — a finding that was robust to subsampling and consistent across both subtypes. Prior work established that cold, dry conditions enhance influenza transmission by prolonging infectious droplet survival in the air and increasing contact rates indoors (\cite{lowen_influenza_2007, frise_contact_2016}), but a direct link between ambient climate and the rate of molecular evolution of circulating strains had not been demonstrated at the global scale. Our findings provide the evidence suggesting that cold local climates act as accelerators of HA evolution. 

This result is consistent with several converging biological mechanisms. Cold and dry conditions reduce mucociliary transport velocity (\cite{kelly_rapid_2021, kilgour_mucociliary_2004}) and lower airway surface liquid pH (\cite{kudo_low_2019}), prolonging the window of extracellular exposure during which virions must resist inactivation before reaching the respiratory epithelium. HA activation is strongly pH-dependent: variants that activate at lower pH thresholds are more stable in mildly acidic extracellular environments, conferring a selective transmission advantage (\cite{russier_molecular_2016, singanayagam_influenza_2019}), while excessive acidification can prematurely trigger HA and render virions non-infectious (\cite{tosheva_hemagglutinin_2023}) — suggesting the airway surface environment actively filters the viral population for pH-stable variants. Concurrently, cold and dry conditions prolong aerosol persistence and impair innate antiviral defences (\cite{lowen_influenza_2007, kudo_low_2019})— which, in combination with reduced mucociliary clearance, widens the effective transmission bottleneck (\cite{sobel_leonard_transmission_2017}), allowing a larger proportion of within-host viral diversity to be transmitted. Together, these conditions expose a larger effective viral population to selection at each transmission event, plausibly elevating the observed substitution rate at the population level without any change in the per-replication error rate.

The temperature signal is specific to haemagglutinin and absent in neuraminidase and the internal control gene PA, a pattern that warrants explanation. HA is the primary target of neutralising antibody responses and evolves under the strongest immune-driven positive selection of any influenza gene (\cite{bush_positive_1999, nelson_evolution_2007}), meaning it has the greatest capacity to accumulate variation that is not immediately purged by purifying selection. This high baseline rate may make HA the most sensitive indicator of any climate-linked increase in mutational supply: the same absolute increase in within-host diversity passed across a transmission bottleneck would produce a detectable rate shift in HA while remaining below the noise threshold in a slower-evolving gene. Beyond this statistical argument, HA faces a qualitatively different selective environment from NA. Virions must traverse the mucosal surface and resist extracellular inactivation before cell entry — exposing HA to selection pressures mediated directly by airway temperature, pH, and mucus viscosity, the very conditions modulated by local climate. NA, by contrast, mediates virion release from already-infected cells and receptor cleavage to prevent self-aggregation (\cite{guo_kinetic_2018}) — a function buffered by the intracellular and membrane-proximal environment rather than the extracellular milieu. The HA–NA functional balance (\cite{guo_kinetic_2018}) may additionally constrain NA's freedom to drift: as HA evolves under climate-associated pressure, compensatory epistatic changes in NA would suppress its net substitution rate rather than elevate it. The PA null result confirms that the pipeline is not generating spurious climate associations in slowly evolving genes, and that the NA null result is biologically interpretable rather than a power failure — NA sample sizes are comparable to HA throughout. One important caveat is that we cannot rule out a simpler explanation: HA has the highest absolute evolutionary rate in this dataset, and a proportionally small climate effect may simply be easier to detect against a fast-moving background. Distinguishing these explanations would require within-subtype analyses stratified by evolutionary rate, which we leave as a direction for future work.

The temperature association is nominally significant in both subtypes, but reaches significance more consistently in H1N1 than H3N2 across subregion analyses and jackknife resamples. This likely reflects differences in tree topology rather than a fundamental biological distinction: post-pandemic H1N1 phylogenies are characterised by a ladder-like, funnel topology with limited clade diversification, while H3N2 maintains several antigenically distinct co-circulating clades (Supplementary Figure X). The between-clade rate heterogeneity in H3N2 dilutes a climate signal that is presumably distributed evenly across lineages, reducing statistical sensitivity without implying the effect is absent. The consistency of sign across both subtypes supports a shared underlying mechanism; the difference in robustness is an artefact of tree structure.

The negative direction of the temperature association is consistent with well-established patterns of influenza seasonality and can be understood through several converging mechanisms that collectively increase the quantity of viral diversity transmitted under cold conditions. Cold and dry air prolongs infectious aerosol survival (\cite{lowen_influenza_2007, shaman_absolute_2009}), increasing transmission frequency and the size of the exposed viral population. Cold temperatures additionally extend the duration of peak viral shedding (\cite{lowen_influenza_2007}) and impair innate antiviral defences in the nasal epithelium (\cite{huang_cold_2023, kudo_low_2019}), promoting more severe and prolonged infections. More severe donor infections, in turn, are associated with a wider transmission bottleneck — more virions transferred per transmission event (\cite{sobel_leonard_transmission_2017, frise_contact_2016} — meaning a larger fraction of within-host viral diversity survives to seed the next infection. The cumulative effect of more frequent transmission events, longer infectious periods, and wider bottlenecks is an increase in the number of replication rounds per transmission chain, and therefore in the number of mutations that accumulate before the lineage is sampled. Critically, this interpretation implies that cold climate elevates the quantity of molecular change rather than its character — consistent with the absence of a temperature effect on $\omega$ in our data, and with the established view that per-replication error rate is an intrinsic property of the viral RdRp, determined by the intracellular biochemical environment rather than ambient conditions (\cite{partlow_influenza_2025}).

Temperature predicts the overall substitution rate of HA but has no detectable effect on $\omega$, indicating that cold climate increases the quantity of molecular change without altering the balance between positive and purifying selection. This pattern is most parsimoniously explained through the lens of effective population size. The effective population size of influenza A at transmission — the number of independently contributing viral lineages that found a new infection — has been estimated at approximately 100–200 (\cite{poon_quantifying_2016}), far below the census count of virions within a host. Under cold conditions, the transmission bottleneck widens (\cite{sobel_leonard_transmission_2017, frise_contact_2016} ), meaning more of the donor's within-host diversity is passed to the recipient, increasing the founding Ne of each new infection. A higher founding Ne means more distinct variants replicate, accumulate further mutations, and are present at the time of sampling — producing longer terminal branches and a higher apparent substitution rate, without any change in polymerase fidelity or the selective pressures acting on the virus. Both synonymous and non-synonymous mutations benefit equally from this wider transmission filter, leaving $\omega$ unchanged. The following analysis of recurrent amino acid substitutions provides further evidence that cold climate acts as a rate accelerator rather than a selective force.

Consistent with the absence of a temperature effect on $\omega$, analysis of recurrent non-synonymous substitutions in the antigenic ridge and epitope regions reveals no enrichment of any specific amino acid change in colder or drier climates, despite several positions showing multiple independent origins across the tree. This is consistent with the established view that antigenic evolution proceeds through discrete, immune-driven cluster transitions (\cite{smith_mapping_2004}) in which competing antigenic lineages are resolved by host population immunity rather than environmental conditions (\cite{strelkowa_clonal_2012}). Climate does not deterministically channel HA toward particular antigenic variants — it increases the supply of raw mutational diversity without biasing the direction of change. The practical implication is probabilistic: a higher substitution rate elevates the chance that an immune-escape variant arises in any given season, but cold climate acts as a rate modulator rather than a selective filter, a conclusion that converges with the dN/dS result above.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. Most fundamentally, the climate predictor is the monthly mean temperature and humidity at the sequence collection site — a reasonable approximation of the transmission environment but not a direct measure of the conditions experienced within the airway or household during infection. The mechanisms we propose, particularly bottleneck widening and impaired mucociliary clearance, operate at scales far below what ERA5 captures, and transmission dynamics are further shaped by household structure and social contact networks (\cite{cauchemez_role_2011}) that aggregate out entirely at the resolution of our data. Testing these mechanisms directly would require paired within-host deep-sequencing and microenvironmental data that are not available at the global scale. This is an inherent trade-off of working with global surveillance data: the geographic and temporal breadth that makes the climate signal detectable in the first place precludes the fine-grained resolution needed to verify the underlying mechanism. Global genomic surveillance datasets such as GISAID are also geographically uneven, with submissions heavily concentrated in North America, Europe, and East Asia — precisely the regions with the most pronounced winter temperature seasonality. This concentration could inflate the apparent climate signal or introduce geographic confounders that sampling density, included as a covariate, only partially addresses. Africa, South and Southeast Asia, and Latin America — regions with distinct climate profiles and high influenza burden — remain substantially underrepresented, and it is not yet known whether the temperature association we report holds in tropical or subtropical settings (\cite{kimura_decoding_2026}). Temporally, collection year is controlled as a fixed effect, but long-term within-subtype trends such as clade replacements may not be fully captured, particularly in H3N2 where clade turnover is rapid. Population density, derived from the GHSL at 5-year epochs, is a static proxy for what is in reality a dynamic driver of transmission; finer-resolution mobility data would be a meaningful improvement. Finally, reverse causality is unlikely — the climate predictor is contemporaneous with collection rather than prospective — but the absence of lagged climate variables means we cannot resolve whether the critical exposure window precedes sampling by days, weeks, or the length of a transmission chain.

If cold local climates accelerate HA evolution, global climate change has the potential to reshape the geographic landscape of influenza evolutionary dynamics. Regions where winters are shortening may see reduced intensity of the temperature-driven rate signal, while subtropical regions experiencing shifts toward drier, cooler conditions could emerge as new evolutionary hotspots (\cite{baker_implications_2021}). Either scenario has direct implications for vaccine strain selection: faster antigenic evolution in particular regions or seasons increases the probability that the circulating strain diverges antigenically from the vaccine before the season peaks, reducing vaccine effectiveness. A logical extension of this work would incorporate lagged climate variables — reflecting the transmission chain in the weeks prior to sampling rather than the collection month alone — as well as within-country climate variation and longitudinal host immunity data, to better resolve the exposure window and disentangle climate effects from immune-driven selection. The value of integrating genomic data with rich demographic registers has been demonstrated compellingly at the national scale, where near-complete genomic sampling of SARS-CoV-2 in Denmark revealed age-, vaccination-, and geography-specific drivers of molecular evolution that would be invisible in a globally aggregated dataset (\cite{khurana_high-resolution_2024}). Extending similar data infrastructure to influenza surveillance globally would enable the kind of individual-level analysis needed to move from the ecological associations we report here toward mechanistic inference. Taken together, our findings underscore the value of globally sampled genomic surveillance for characterising the environmental drivers of pathogen evolution — and point toward a future in which climate projections and genomic epidemiology can be jointly used to anticipate where novel influenza variants are most likely to emerge. 

Using a global sample of influenza A H1N1 and H3N2 sequences spanning a decade and two subtypes, we demonstrate a robust negative association between local environmental temperature and haemagglutinin evolutionary rate — an effect that is consistent in direction across subtypes, gene regions, and subsamples, and absent in neuraminidase and the internal control PA. The signal is one of rate, not character: cold climates accelerate HA's molecular clock without shifting the balance of selection, consistent with a mechanism of wider transmission bottlenecks and larger effective viral population sizes rather than altered polymerase fidelity or directional immune pressure. This establishes local climate as a modulator of influenza's evolutionary tempo — not a deterministic driver of antigenic novelty, but a condition that raises the probability of novel variant emergence by increasing the pace of molecular change. As climate patterns shift globally, so too may the geographic distribution of evolutionary hotspots, and the integration of genomic surveillance with environmental data offers a principled framework for anticipating where those hotspots will be.


\section*{Methods}

This analysis used a global sample of HA, NA and PA from H1N1 and H3N2 subtypes, collected between 2009-2019. Originally passaged sequences matching this criteria were obtained from GISAID EpiFlu along with their metadata detailing their location and collection date (\cite{shu_gisaid_2017}). Location strings found in the metadata were geocoded to latitude and longitude via Nominatim (OpenStreetMap) (\cite{noauthor_architecture_nodate, noauthor_openstreetmap_nodate}) with a hierarchical fallback to coarser geographic levels. For each date and location, a monthly mean temperature(2-m air) and 2-m dewpoint temperature was fetched from the Copernicus Climate Data Store at a $~0.1^\circ$ spatial resolution (\cite{noauthor_era5_nodate}). Relative humidity was derived from temperature and dewpoint using the Magnus formula. $\Delta\text{T}$ and $\Delta\text{RH}$ were computed as the difference between the collection month and the preceding month at the same location. Population density was extracted from the Global Human Settlement Layer (GHSL R2023A, 100m resolution, Mollweide projection) at the geocoded sample coordinates with the GHSL epoch (2010/2015/2020) matched to the sample collection year (\cite{pesaresi_advances_2024}). To control for uneven global surveillance effort, sampling density was computed as the count of sequences in the dataset sharing the same location and collection year and month. 

Sequences were aligned using MAFFT (\cite{katoh_mafft_2013}) and alignment columns with $>$50\% gaps were removed. Duplicated sequences were removed (Table \ref{tab:sequences}).  An initial maximum likelihood phylogeny was inferred with MAPLE v0.6.8 (\textcolor{red}{verify}) (\cite{de_maio_maximum_2022}) and time-calibrated with TreeTime using a relaxed clock and collection dates as calibrations (\cite{sagulenko_treetime_2018}). Sequences identified as clock outliers by TreeTime were excluded and a second MAPLE tree was inferred with the clock-filtered sequence set. The second round MAPLE tree was then time-calibrated with TreeTime once again, using the same relaxed clock.

Pairwise synonymous ($d_\text{S}$) and non-synonymous ($d_\text{N}$) distances were estimated for all sequence pairs using yn00 (\cite{yang_estimating_2000}) implemented in PAML using F3$\times$4 codon frequencies and the standard genetic code. Codon columns containing stop codons or gaps were removed prior to each pairwise calculation. Per-sample mean $d_\text{N}$ and $d_\text{S}$ were computed as row means of the pairwise distance matrices. Samples with numerically unstable rates (log rate $< -$8) were removed (Table \ref{tab:sequences}, column 'Final'). 

To account for concentration of adaptive evolution of hemagglutinin in the antigenic surface sites (\textcolor{red}{insert citation}), the rate of non-synonymous change was analysed separately for the antigenic ridge, the broader epitope regions and the HA2 stalk region, used to infer whether the antigenic regions were driving the signal across the whole gene.  H1 sequence A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 CY009444 (\textcolor{red}{citation}) and H3 sequence A/Aichi/2/1968 CY12168 (\textcolor{red}{citation}) were used as references to map the positions of gene subregions (\textcolor{red}{Supp figure: gene maps}), which were then extracted from the full MAFFT alignment. For each tip, the ancestral codon at each position was inferred by maximum parsimony using phangorn (\cite{schliep_phangorn_2011}).  The tip codon was then compared to its inferred ancestor and classified as synonymous or non-synonymous. (\textcolor{red}{mention why we didn't use yn00 for this}). Stop codons, gaps, and ambiguous bases were excluded. The per-tip non-synonymous rate was calculated by dividing the count of non-synonymous changes with the time of the branch (\textcolor{red}{phrase this better}). Sequences not containing a non-synonymous change in the region undergoing analyses were removed. (\textcolor{red}{maybe have another table showing that}) 

Phylogenetic linear models were fitted with phylolm (\cite{ho_phylolm_2020}) while estimating Pagel's  $\lambda$
covariance. Analyses on the whole gene included two response variables: log overall evolutionary rate and log $\omega$, defined as  $\omega = d_\text{N} / d_\text{S}$. Analyses on the gene subregions used log non-synonymous substitution rate per subregion (antigenic ridge, epitope, HA2). Predictors used in all analyses were temperature [$^\circ$C], relative humidity [\%], $\Delta$-temperature, $\Delta$-humidity, sampling density (log-transformed), population density (log-transformed) and collection year as factor, controlling for secular trends. All phylolm results underwent Benjamini-Hochberg FDR corrections and associations with $p_\text{FDR} < 0.05$ were considered significant. 

Recurrent non-synonymous mutations (independent occurrences $>$ 5) were tested for association with each climate variable using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The effect size was quantified as the rank-biserial correlation. Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction was applied within each gene and region. 

To asses whether the temperature association was robust to subsampling, a block jackknife was performed: 1000 year-stratified random subsamples of 1000 sequences (or, in the subregion cases, where N dropped below 1000, at 60\% of N) were drawn. The tree was pruned to each subsample and phylolm was refitted using a reduced formula where log substitution rate or non-synonymous rate (in the case of subregion analyses) was used as a response variable and scaled temperature and collection year as factor were used as predictors. The distribution of temperature coefficients across the 1000 subsamples, and the proportion reaching $p<0.05$ were used to confirm that the full-model signal was not driven by any sequence or temporal cluster.
