\section*{Significance statememt}
Seasonal influenza causes hundreds of thousands of deaths annually, yet predicting which viral strains will circulate each season remains difficult. This study reveals that local temperature directly shapes how fast influenza A evolves its surface proteins — the targets of our immune system and vaccines. By linking local temperature conditions to viral evolution, we identify a previously unknown factor influencing strain emergence. These findings suggest that seasonal models incorporating temperature could improve forecasts of dominant flu strains, informing vaccine composition decisions months in advance. Our results open pathways for climate-informed pandemic preparedness and underscore how environmental conditions shape pathogen evolution.

\section*{Abstract}
Influenza A displays marked seasonal patterns of infection in temperate regions, yet the extent to which local climatic conditions influence viral evolutionary dynamics remains unknown. Cold and dry conditions promote viral transmission, yet no work has explored relationships between influenza A evolution and local climate. Here, we analysed a global sample of H1N1 and H3N2 influenza A isolated between 2009–2019, examining associations between local temperature, relative humidity, population density, and terminal-branch evolutionary rates of the hemagglutinin and neuraminidase viral surface proteins, alongside the constrained polymerase acidic protein. We found a marked negative association between hemagglutinin evolution rates and temperature across both subtypes. However, individual mutations did not preferentially occur under particular environmental conditions. We propose this reflects looser transmission bottlenecks promoted by increased droplet survival in cold air and impaired host airway conditions. Our results establish local temperature as a modulator of influenza A hemagglutinin evolutionary tempo, highlighting the importance of climate context when anticipating novel seasonal strains.


\section*{Introduction}

Influenza is one of the most significant infectious diseases affecting humans, causing recurrent seasonal epidemics and occasional pandemics with substantial health and socioeconomic impacts (\cite{hsieh_influenza_2006, trovao_when_2020}). Influenza A infections display marked seasonal patterns, peaking in winter months in temperate regions and in rainy seasons in the tropics (\cite{petrova_evolution_2018}). Local climates are likely to be involved in variant emergence through influencing modes of transmission, host immunity and respiratory tract conditions (\cite{kudo_low_2019}). One mechanism by which climate could influence viral evolution is through transmission bottlenecks. Climatic conditions that allow greater droplet stability can relax the population bottleneck at transmission (\cite{frise_contact_2016}), reducing the loss of novel variants during host-to-host spread. Experimental work has shown that colder and dry conditions increase transmission efficiency due to enhanced virus survival in airborne droplets (\cite{lowen_influenza_2007, shaman_absolute_2009}). Meanwhile, hot and humid conditions might also promote viral survival by reducing dessication (\cite{minhaz_ud-dean_structural_2010}), such that the role of climate on variant emergence is likely complex (\cite{deyle_global_2016}) and remains poorly understood. Recent expansions in global influenza surveillance have generated extensive collections of viral genome sequences accompanied by sampling dates and locations. In particular, sequences of the antigenically important hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA) surface glycoproteins are routinely collected worldwide, providing an unprecedented opportunity to investigate environmental correlates of influenza evolutionary dynamics. These data enable tests of whether local climatic conditions are associated with rates of viral evolution across diverse geographic settings and whether climate may contribute to patterns of influenza strain emergence (\cite{volz_viral_2013}). 

The influenza surface proteins HA and NA are fast-evolving and central to infection and immune escape (\cite{nelson_evolution_2007}). HA mediates host cell entry by binding sialic acid receptors, while NA facilitates virion release by cleaving sialic acids from host glycoproteins (\cite{matsuoka_comprehensive_2013}). Both proteins mediate key stages of the viral life cycle that occur outside host cells and are sensitive to the physicochemical conditions that vary with local climate (\cite{klenow_influenza_2023, tosheva_hemagglutinin_2023}), raising the possibility that that climatic conditions influence the relative fitness of circulating HA and NA variants. Environmental temperature and humidity may further influence evolutionary rates indirectly, through altered transmission dynamics, infection duration, and viral loads. Alternatively, if evolution is dominated by the intracellular biochemical environment (\cite{partlow_influenza_2025}) or by demographic patterns of contact and mobility (\cite{cauchemez_role_2011, endo_within_2021}), outdoor climate may be a weak or negligible predictor of molecular evolutionary rate.

In RNA viruses like influenza A, error-prone replication continuously generates mutations whose fate is determined by replication dynamics, population bottlenecks, and immune selection (\cite{nelson_evolution_2007, wille_ecology_2020}). Progressive accumulation of mutations at immune-targeted sites of HA and NA, known as the antigenic drift, is a potent driver of novel strain emergence and reduces vaccine efficacy by allowing circulating strains to escape immunity elicited by prior infection or vaccination (\cite{bedford_integrating_2014}). Antigenic shift, arising from reassortment between co-circulating lineages, can additionally introduce antigenically novel subtypes with pandemic potential (\cite{nelson_evolution_2007}). Environmental conditions may influence viral evolutionary dynamics through effects on virion survival, transmission bottlenecks, and host susceptibility. Global climate change might therefore alter the prevalence and geographic distribution of evolutionary hotspots, shifting from the seasonal peaks currently observed in temperate regions toward more persistent or geographically diffuse outbreaks (\cite{baker_implications_2021}).

In this study, we use a global dataset of influenza A sequences to test for associations between local climate, population density, and rates of molecular evolution of surface antigens across the two dominant seasonal subtypes, H1N1 and H3N2. The internal polymerase acidic protein gene (PA), a slow-evolving internal gene, serves as a negative control. Using between 7,500 and 12,800 sequences per gene segment (totalling $\sim$60,000 across six segments), we estimated terminal branch rates and synonymous and non-synonymous substitution rates phylogenetically, and linked each sample to ERA5-Land climate data and demographic covariates. Phylogenetic comparative regression was then used to test whether local temperature and humidity predict HA and NA evolutionary rates — and, if so, whether climate acts as a rate accelerator, a directional selector, or both.

\section*{Results}

To assess whether local climate conditions shape influenza A molecular evolution, we applied phylogenetic comparative regression to substitution rates estimated from H1N1 and H3N2 sequences collected between 2009 and 2019 across 168 countries (Figure \ref{fig:main}A). Surface proteins HA and NA were analysed for their fast-evolving nature and central role in immune escape, while the internal polymerase acidic protein (PA) served as a negative control.

To account for the uneven seasonal distribution of influenza surveillance, sampling density was included as a covariate. Temperature was negatively associated with HA substitution rate across both subtypes (Figure \ref{fig:main}C); this effect was not observed in NA or PA.

To examine whether temperature affects the selective regime rather than the overall mutation supply, we analysed synonymous ($d_{\text{S}}$) and non-synonymous ($d_{\text{N}}$) substitution rates separately across the whole gene, the stalk region (HA2), the epitope regions, and the antigenic ridge (\textcolor{red}{Supp figure gene map}). The regression coefficients for $d_{\text{N}}$, $d_{\text{S}}$, and overall substitution rate were consistent in direction and magnitude across both subtypes (Figure \ref{fig:main}D \textcolor{red}{supplementary forest fig}), indicating that temperature modulates the rate of molecular change without shifting the balance between positive and purifying selection. The temperature signal persisted across all gene regions in H1N1 but did not reach significance in the subregion analyses of H3N2, likely reflecting the reduced statistical power inherent in partitioning the dataset by gene region, compounded by the weaker overall signal in H3N2 relative to H1N1. 

The block jackknife sensitivity analysis confirmed that the temperature signal in hemagglutinin was not driven by any individual sequence or sequence cluster: temperature retained a significant negative effect on H1 substitution rate in 52\% of subsamples (Figure \ref{fig:main}E; \textcolor{red}{Supp figure per-sequence jackknives}). The corresponding figure for H3 was 10\%, consistent with its lower overall significance rather than indicating the effect is absent.

\section*{Discussion}

In a large global sample of influenza A H1N1 and H3N2 sequences, we reveal a robust negative association between local environmental temperature and hemagglutinin evolutionary rate, a finding that was robust to subsampling and consistent across both subtypes. Prior work established that cold, dry conditions enhance influenza transmission by prolonging infectious droplet survival in the air and increasing contact rates indoors (\cite{lowen_influenza_2007, frise_contact_2016}), but a direct link between ambient climate and the rate of molecular evolution of circulating strains had not been demonstrated at the global scale. Our findings provide evidence suggesting that cold local climates act as accelerators of HA evolution. 

This result is consistent with several converging biological mechanisms. Cold and dry conditions reduce mucociliary transport velocity (\cite{kelly_rapid_2021, kilgour_mucociliary_2004}) and lower airway surface liquid pH (\cite{kudo_low_2019}), prolonging the window of extracellular exposure during which virions must resist inactivation before reaching the respiratory epithelium. HA activation is strongly pH-dependent: variants that activate at lower pH thresholds are more stable in mildly acidic extracellular environments, conferring a selective transmission advantage (\cite{russier_molecular_2016, singanayagam_influenza_2019}), while excessive acidification can prematurely trigger HA and render virions non-infectious (\cite{tosheva_hemagglutinin_2023}), suggesting the airway surface environment actively filters the viral population for pH-stable variants. Concurrently, cold and dry conditions prolong aerosol persistence and impair innate antiviral defences (\cite{lowen_influenza_2007, kudo_low_2019}), which, in combination with reduced mucociliary clearance, widens the effective transmission bottleneck (\cite{sobel_leonard_transmission_2017}), allowing a larger proportion of within-host viral diversity to be transmitted. Together, these conditions expose a larger effective viral population to selection at each transmission event, plausibly elevating the observed substitution rate at the population level without any change in the per-replication error rate.

The temperature signal is specific to hemagglutinin and absent in neuraminidase and the internal control gene PA, a pattern that warrants explanation. HA is the primary target of neutralising antibody responses and evolves under the strongest immune-driven positive selection of any influenza gene (\cite{bush_positive_1999, nelson_evolution_2007}), meaning it has the greatest capacity to accumulate variation that is not immediately purged by purifying selection. This high baseline rate may make HA the most sensitive indicator of any climate-linked increase in mutational supply: the same absolute increase in within-host diversity passed across a transmission bottleneck would produce a detectable rate shift in HA while remaining below the noise threshold in a slower-evolving gene. Beyond this statistical argument, HA faces a qualitatively different selective environment from NA. Virions must traverse the mucosal surface and resist extracellular inactivation before cell entry, exposing HA to selection pressures mediated directly by airway temperature, pH, and mucus viscosity, the very conditions modulated by local climate. NA, by contrast, mediates virion release from already-infected cells and receptor cleavage to prevent self-aggregation (\cite{guo_kinetic_2018}), a function buffered by the intracellular and membrane-proximal environment rather than the extracellular milieu. The HA–NA functional balance (\cite{guo_kinetic_2018}) may additionally constrain NA's freedom to drift: as HA evolves under climate-associated pressure, compensatory epistatic changes in NA would suppress its net substitution rate rather than elevate it. The PA null result confirms that the pipeline is not generating spurious climate associations in slowly evolving genes, and that the NA null result is biologically interpretable rather than a power failure — NA sample sizes are comparable to HA throughout. One important caveat is that we cannot rule out a simpler explanation: HA has the highest absolute evolutionary rate in this dataset, and a proportionally small climate effect may simply be easier to detect against a fast-moving background. Distinguishing these explanations would require within-subtype analyses stratified by evolutionary rate, which we leave as a direction for future work.

The temperature association is nominally significant in both subtypes, but reaches significance more consistently in H1N1 than H3N2 across subregion analyses and jackknife resamples (\ref{fig:main} D, E, \textcolor{red}{Supp jackknife}). This likely reflects differences in tree topology rather than a fundamental biological distinction: post-pandemic H1N1 phylogenies are characterised by a ladder-like, funnel topology with limited clade diversification, while H3N2 maintains several antigenically distinct co-circulating clades (\textcolor{red}{Supp figure: trees}). The between-clade rate heterogeneity in H3N2 dilutes a climate signal that is presumably distributed evenly across lineages, reducing statistical sensitivity without implying the effect is absent. The consistency of sign across both subtypes supports a shared underlying mechanism; the difference in robustness is an artefact of tree structure.

The negative direction of the temperature association is consistent with well-established patterns of influenza seasonality and can be understood through several converging mechanisms that collectively increase the quantity of viral diversity transmitted under cold conditions. Cold and dry air prolongs infectious aerosol survival (\cite{lowen_influenza_2007, shaman_absolute_2009}), increasing transmission frequency and the size of the exposed viral population. Cold temperatures additionally extend the duration of peak viral shedding (\cite{lowen_influenza_2007}) and impair innate antiviral defences in the nasal epithelium (\cite{huang_cold_2023, kudo_low_2019}), promoting more severe and prolonged infections. More severe donor infections, in turn, are associated with a wider transmission bottleneck. As more virions are transferred per transmission event (\cite{sobel_leonard_transmission_2017, frise_contact_2016}), a larger fraction of within-host viral diversity survives to seed the next infection. The cumulative effect of more frequent transmission events, longer infectious periods, and wider bottlenecks is an increase in the number of replication rounds per transmission chain, and therefore in the number of mutations that accumulate before the lineage is sampled. Critically, this interpretation implies that cold climate elevates the quantity of molecular change rather than its character, which is consistent with the absence of a temperature effect on $\omega$ in our data, and with the established view that per-replication error rate is an intrinsic property of the viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, determined by the intracellular biochemical environment rather than ambient conditions (\cite{partlow_influenza_2025}).

Temperature predicts the overall substitution rate of HA but has no detectable effect on $\omega$, indicating that cold climate increases the quantity of molecular change without altering the balance between positive and purifying selection. This pattern is most parsimoniously explained through the lens of effective population size. The effective population size ($Ne$), defined as the number of independently contributing viral lineages that found a new infection, has been estimated at approximately 100-200 for influenza A (\cite{poon_quantifying_2016}). Under cold conditions, the transmission bottleneck widens (\cite{sobel_leonard_transmission_2017, frise_contact_2016}), meaning more of the donor's within-host diversity is passed to the recipient, increasing the founding $Ne$ of each new infection. A higher founding $Ne$ means more distinct variants replicate, accumulate further mutations, and are present at the time of sampling, producing longer terminal branches and a higher apparent substitution rate, without any change in polymerase fidelity or the selective pressures acting on the virus. Both synonymous and non-synonymous mutations benefit equally from this wider transmission filter, leaving $\omega$ unchanged. 

Consistent with the absence of a temperature effect on $\omega$, analysis of recurrent non-synonymous substitutions in the antigenic ridge and epitope regions reveals no enrichment of any specific amino acid change in colder or drier climates, despite several positions showing multiple independent origins across the tree (\ref{fig:mutations}). This is consistent with the established view that antigenic evolution proceeds through discrete, immune-driven cluster transitions (\cite{smith_mapping_2004}) in which competing antigenic lineages are resolved by host population immunity rather than environmental conditions (\cite{strelkowa_clonal_2012}). Climate does not deterministically channel HA toward particular antigenic variants, but rather it increases the supply of raw mutational diversity without biasing the direction of change. The practical implication is probabilistic: a higher substitution rate elevates the chance that an immune-escape variant arises in any given season, but cold climate acts as a rate modulator rather than a selective filter, a conclusion that converges with the dN/dS result above.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. Most fundamentally, the climate predictor is the monthly mean temperature and humidity at the sequence collection site — a reasonable approximation of the transmission environment but not a direct measure of the conditions experienced within the airway or household during infection. The mechanisms we propose, particularly bottleneck widening and impaired mucociliary clearance, operate at scales far below what ERA5 captures, and transmission dynamics are further shaped by household structure and social contact networks (\cite{cauchemez_role_2011}) that aggregate out entirely at the resolution of our data. Testing these mechanisms directly would require paired within-host deep-sequencing and microenvironmental data that are not available at the global scale. This is an inherent trade-off of working with global surveillance data: the geographic and temporal breadth that makes the climate signal detectable in the first place precludes the fine-grained resolution needed to verify the underlying mechanism. Global genomic surveillance datasets such as GISAID are also geographically uneven, with submissions heavily concentrated in North America, Europe, and East Asia. This concentration could inflate the apparent climate signal or introduce geographic confounders that sampling density, included as a covariate, only partially addresses. Africa, South and Southeast Asia, and Latin America — regions with distinct climate profiles and high influenza burden — remain substantially underrepresented, and it is not yet known whether the temperature association we report holds in tropical or subtropical settings (\cite{kimura_decoding_2026}). Temporally, collection year is controlled as a fixed effect, but long-term within-subtype trends such as clade replacements may not be fully captured, particularly in H3N2 where clade turnover is rapid. Population density, derived from the GHSL at 5-year epochs, is a static proxy for what is in reality a dynamic driver of transmission; finer-resolution mobility data would be a meaningful improvement. Finally, reverse causality is unlikely — the climate predictor is contemporaneous with collection rather than prospective — but the absence of lagged climate variables means we cannot resolve whether the critical exposure window precedes sampling by days, weeks, or the length of a transmission chain.

If cold local climates accelerate HA evolution, global climate change has the potential to reshape the geographic landscape of influenza evolutionary dynamics. Regions where winters are shortening may see reduced intensity of the temperature-driven rate signal, while subtropical regions experiencing shifts toward drier, cooler conditions could emerge as new evolutionary hotspots (\cite{baker_implications_2021}). Either scenario has direct implications for vaccine strain selection: faster antigenic evolution in particular regions or seasons increases the probability that the circulating strain diverges antigenically from the vaccine before the season peaks, reducing vaccine effectiveness. A logical extension of this work would incorporate lagged climate variables, reflecting the transmission chain in the weeks prior to sampling rather than the collection month alone, as well as within-country climate variation and longitudinal host immunity data, to better resolve the exposure window and disentangle climate effects from immune-driven selection. The value of integrating genomic data with rich demographic registers has been demonstrated compellingly at the national scale, where near-complete genomic sampling of SARS-CoV-2 in Denmark revealed age-, vaccination-, and geography-specific drivers of molecular evolution that would be invisible in a globally aggregated dataset (\cite{khurana_high-resolution_2024}). Extending similar data infrastructure to influenza surveillance globally would enable the kind of individual-level analysis needed to move from the ecological associations we report here toward mechanistic inference. Taken together, our findings underscore the value of globally sampled genomic surveillance for characterising the environmental drivers of pathogen evolution and point toward a future in which climate projections and genomic epidemiology can be jointly used to anticipate novel influenza A variant emergence. 

Using a global sample of influenza A H1N1 and H3N2 sequences spanning a decade, we demonstrate a robust negative association between local environmental temperature and hemagglutinin evolutionary rate- This effect is consistent in direction across subtypes, gene regions, and subsamples, and absent in neuraminidase and the internal control PA. The signal is one of rate, not character: cold climates accelerate HA's molecular clock without shifting the balance of selection, consistent with a mechanism of wider transmission bottlenecks and larger effective viral population sizes rather than altered polymerase fidelity or directional immune pressure. This establishes local climate as a modulator of influenza's evolutionary tempo — not a deterministic driver of antigenic novelty, but a condition that raises the probability of novel variant emergence by increasing the pace of molecular change. As climate patterns shift globally, so too may the geographic distribution of evolutionary hotspots, and the integration of genomic surveillance with environmental data offers a principled framework for anticipating where those hotspots will be.


\section*{Methods}

Originally passaged HA, NA, and PA sequences from H1N1 and H3N2 subtypes collected between 2009 and 2019 were obtained from GISAID EpiFlu (\cite{shu_gisaid_2017}). Collection site coordinates were assigned by geocoding the location strings in the GISAID metadata via Nominatim (OpenStreetMap) (\cite{noauthor_architecture_nodate, noauthor_openstreetmap_nodate}), with hierarchical fallback to coarser geographic levels when precise geocoding failed. For each sample, monthly mean 2-m air temperature and 2-m dewpoint temperature were extracted from the ERA5-Land reanalysis at ${\sim}0.1^\circ$ spatial resolution via the Copernicus Climate Data Store (\cite{noauthor_era5_nodate}), and relative humidity was derived from these using the Magnus formula. $\Delta$T and $\Delta$RH were computed as the difference between the collection month and the preceding month at the same location. Population density was extracted from the Global Human Settlement Layer (GHSL R2023A, 100 m resolution, Mollweide projection) at each sample's geocoded coordinates, with the GHSL epoch (2010/2015/2020) (\cite{pesaresi_advances_2024}) matched to the collection year. Sampling density, defined as the count of sequences in the dataset sharing the same location and collection date (year, month), was included as a covariate to control for uneven global surveillance effort.

Sequences were aligned with MAFFT (\cite{katoh_mafft_2013}) and alignment columns with $>$50\% gaps were removed. Duplicate sequences were removed prior to phylogenetic inference (Table \ref{tab:sequences}). An initial maximum-likelihood phylogeny was inferred with MAPLE v0.6.8 (\cite{de_maio_maximum_2022}) and time-calibrated with TreeTime using a relaxed clock and collection dates as tip calibrations (\cite{sagulenko_treetime_2018}). Sequences identified as clock outliers by TreeTime were excluded, and a second MAPLE tree was inferred from the clock-filtered dataset and time-calibrated with TreeTime under the same settings.

Pairwise synonymous ($d_\text{S}$) and non-synonymous ($d_\text{N}$) distances were estimated for all sequence pairs using yn00 (\cite{yang_estimating_2000}) from the PAML package, with F3$\times$4 codon frequencies and the standard genetic code. Codon columns containing stop codons or gaps were removed prior to each pairwise calculation. Per-sample mean $d_\text{N}$ and $d_\text{S}$ were computed as row means of the pairwise distance matrices. Samples with numerically unstable rates (log rate $< -$8) were removed (Table \ref{tab:sequences}, column `Final').

To account for the concentration of adaptive evolution in the antigenic surface sites of hemagglutinin (\cite{bush_positive_1999}), non-synonymous substitution rates were estimated separately for the antigenic ridge, the broader epitope regions, and the HA2 stalk region. H1 reference sequence A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 (CY009444) and H3 reference sequence A/Aichi/2/1968 (CY12168) (\cite{bao_influenza_2008}) were used to map the positions of gene subregions onto the full MAFFT alignment (\textcolor{red}{Supp figure: gene maps}), from which each region was extracted. For each tip, the ancestral codon at each position was inferred by maximum parsimony using phangorn (\cite{schliep_phangorn_2011}), and the tip codon was compared to its inferred ancestor and classified as synonymous or non-synonymous. This approach was used preferentially over pairwise yn00 distances for its ability to be applied to arbitrary subsets of codons. Stop codons, gaps, and ambiguous bases were excluded. The per-tip non-synonymous rate was calculated as the count of non-synonymous changes in the region divided by the terminal branch length. Sequences lacking a non-synonymous change in the analysed region were excluded from subregion analyses. \textcolor{red}{(Supplementary table)}

Phylogenetic linear models were fitted with phylolm (\cite{ho_phylolm_2020}), estimating Pagel's $\lambda$ to account for phylogenetic signal in the residuals. Whole-gene analyses included two response variables: log overall evolutionary rate and log $\omega$, where $\omega = d_\text{N} / d_\text{S}$. Subregion analyses used log non-synonymous substitution rate for each region (antigenic ridge, epitope regions, HA2). All models included temperature ($^\circ$C), relative humidity (\%), $\Delta$-temperature, $\Delta$-humidity, log sampling density, log population density, and collection year as a factor to control for secular trends. Benjamini--Hochberg FDR correction was applied to all phylolm results; associations with $p_\text{FDR} < 0.05$ were considered statistically significant.

Recurrent non-synonymous substitutions occurring at least five times independently across the phylogeny were tested for association with each climate variable using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with effect size quantified as the rank-biserial correlation. Benjamini--Hochberg FDR correction was applied within each gene and region.

To assess the robustness of the temperature association to subsampling, a block jackknife was performed. Year-stratified random subsamples of 1,000 sequences were drawn 1,000 times (or 60\% of $N$ for subregion analyses where $N < 1{,}000$). The phylogeny was pruned to each subsample and phylolm was refitted with log substitution rate (or log non-synonymous rate for subregion analyses) as the response and scaled temperature and collection year as predictors. The distribution of temperature coefficients across subsamples, and the proportion reaching $p < 0.05$, were used to confirm that the full-model signal was not attributable to any sequence or temporal cluster.
